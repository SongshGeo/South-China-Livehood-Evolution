## Agent-based model

We built a spatially explicit agent-based model of prehistoric livelihood change in
South China. The model asks why farming took hold so slowly in a landscape already
occupied by hunter-gatherers, and it ranks the candidate suppressing mechanisms by
effect size. A simulation is needed rather than an aggregate model because the
mechanism of interest is local. Farming spreads only where a farming group already
sits next to a forager, so the outcome turns on how the two populations are arranged
in space rather than on their totals. The model is process-based and heuristic.
Its parameters are set from archaeological and ethnographic estimates, but none are
fitted to an observed time series. We therefore validate it against qualitative
patterns, not against year-by-year data.

Three livelihoods share one gridded landscape: rainfed (dryland) farmers ($F$),
paddy-rice farmers ($R$), and hunter-gatherers ($H$). Each agent is a settlement
group, not an individual person, and its single state variable is its population
size $N$. A group has a minimum viable size ($N_{\min} = 6$); a group that falls
below it is removed. The landscape is a fixed raster of South China spanning
104.4–121.3° E and 19.2–29.1° N at 5 arc-minute resolution, which gives 6835
modelled cells of about 78 km² each. Each cell carries an elevation, a slope, and a
water class, and holds at most one group. A cell is arable for rainfed farming when
it is gentle ($\le 10^\circ$) and lowland ($0 < z < 200$ m); paddy farming needs a
stricter slope ($\le 0.5^\circ$). This yields 2620 rainfed-arable and 885
paddy-arable cells. Water enters as a property of land rather than as a barrier: the
sea lies outside the modelled grid, and the 1064 cells classed as near-water carry a
forager capacity five times that of inland cells, standing for the richer aquatic
resources they offer. The raster is a static template, so all change is generated by
the agents. A full
description of the model is given in the Supplementary Information. It follows
ODD+D, an extension of the ODD (Overview, Design concepts, Details) protocol that
adds an account of human Decision-making.

## Process overview and scheduling

Time is discrete and has no calendar: one step is an abstract generation-scale
interval, and we read all results as trajectories in step number rather than in
calendar years. Each run lasts $T = 500$ steps, and each step follows a fixed
sequence.

1. **Immigration.** Agriculture enters from outside the region as two independent
   streams. The number of arriving rainfed groups is $m_F \sim
   \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_F)$ and of paddy groups $m_R \sim
   \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_R)$. Each new group is placed on a uniformly random
   empty cell that is arable for its own livelihood, at a size drawn from a
   livelihood-specific range.
2. **Group updates.** Every group already present acts once, in randomised order.
   In its turn a group (i) grows geometrically, $N \leftarrow N\,(1 + r)$; (ii)
   may convert to another livelihood; (iii) may found a colony on a nearby empty
   cell; and (iv) may lose population to mortality. A hunter-gatherer group (v)
   additionally takes one step to a random suitable neighbour if it is still
   mobile. Updates are asynchronous, so a group sees the moves made earlier in
   the same step.
3. **Regional forager ceiling.** After all groups have acted, the total
   hunter-gatherer population is capped at $K_H = k_H\,A$, where $A$ is the number
   of land cells and $k_H$ is a per-cell carrying capacity. If the total exceeds
   $K_H$, forager groups are trimmed in random order until it returns to the
   ceiling.
4. **Recording.** The summed population and the number of groups are recorded for
   each livelihood.

Growth is deterministic; immigration, conversion, colonization, mortality, movement,
and trimming are all stochastic. Every group draws mortality once per step whatever
its livelihood, so livelihoods differ in mortality only through the probability
$p_{\text{loss}}$ of a loss event and the fraction lost when one occurs. Population
is conserved when a colony splits off, except that a parent left below $N_{\min}$ is
removed along with its remainder.

The region starts full of foragers and empty of farmers. Half the land cells carry a
forager group at $t=0$, together holding about 76% of the regional ceiling $K_H$, so
incoming farmers meet a landscape that is already close to saturated. No farming
groups are present at the start, so any farming population in a run is produced
entirely by immigration, growth, and conversion rather than by an initial endowment.

## Growth, capacity, and conversion

A farming group's per-group capacity grows with its cultivated area $a$,
$N_{\max} = \pi a^2 / c$, where $c$ is the land needed per person; a group that
exceeds its capacity intensifies, enlarging $a$ and lowering its growth rate by a
complexity factor $\kappa$. Paddy groups need half the land per person that rainfed
groups do, so they carry twice the capacity on the same area. Hunter-gatherers grow
more slowly than farmers ($r_H < r_F, r_R$) but are held near the regional ceiling
$K_H$ for most of a run, which makes them a persistent competitor for space.

Conversion lets a group change livelihood in place, and it is the mechanism of
central interest because it couples the growth of farming to the standing forager
population. Each conversion is a two-stage decision: the path's conditions must
hold, and then a Bernoulli draw at the path's probability must succeed. Five
directed paths exist; the two that link rainfed farmers and foragers carry the key
tension. A small farming group can revert to foraging with probability
$p_{F\to H}$ (written f2h), which drains it back into the much larger foraging
pool. A forager group can take up farming with probability $p_{H\to F}$
(written h2f), but only when a farming group sits in its neighbourhood, so
agriculture spreads by contact.

## Simulation experiments and analysis

Every experiment repeats each parameter combination five times and runs each
replicate for $500$ steps. We summarise a trajectory by its mean across
replicates with a $95\%$ confidence interval, and a scalar outcome by its end
state, the mean over the final 50 steps of all five replicates. Because sample size
is a design choice here, we report effect sizes and the replicate count rather than
significance tests, and we read the results as relative effect sizes and emergent
patterns rather than as calibrated predictions. We ran four experiments, each
producing one figure.

**Baseline suppression (Figure 2).** Under default parameters we tracked the
agricultural share of total population,
$s_t = (N^F_t + N^R_t)/(N^F_t + N^R_t + N^H_t)$. A low, slowly rising share is the
emergent signature of suppression: foraging keeps most of the region.

**Conversion (Figure 3).** We probed the two farmer–forager paths at three
resolutions: a baseline-versus-off contrast; a coarse sweep of both probabilities
($\{0, 0.02, \dots, 0.10\}$); and a fine eleven-by-eleven grid in the sensitive
corner ($p_{F\to H} \in [0, 0.02]$, $p_{H\to F} \in [0, 0.15]$). We fitted a
separable additive model in log space,
$\log N \approx \alpha(p_{F\to H}) + \beta(p_{H\to F}) + \gamma$, and report its
fit ($R^2 = 0.992$), which shows the two paths act almost independently with
$p_{F\to H}$ dominant.

**Ranking the factors (Figure 4).** We compared three factors on the rainfed
farming trajectory: the rainfed immigration intensity
($\lambda_F \in \{2,4,6,8,10\}$), the forager carrying capacity
($k_H \in \{15,25,35\}$), and the terrain. The terrain factor is a two-by-two
factorial in which elevation and slope are each either the real surface or a
spatially uniform one. Reporting all factors against the same response makes the
effect sizes directly comparable.

**Paddy versus rainfed leverage (Figure 5).** We swept the two immigration
intensities together, normalised each to a multiple of its baseline, and compared
the response of the total agricultural population $N^F + N^R$. The slope of the
end-state response measures each stream's leverage.

We did not run a global variance-based sensitivity analysis. The sweeps are
one- and two-factor grids chosen around the mechanism of interest, and the
conversion surface is dense enough in that corner to characterise the interaction
directly; the remaining parameters are held at their sourced values.

## Software

The model is implemented in Python (3.11) on an agent-based modelling framework
(ABSESpy 0.11). Model structure is separated from parameters, so sweeps are run by
changing configuration only. Each combination is run as an independent batch of five
replicates, and each replicate writes the population and group-count time series
that feed the figures. Every replicate derives its own seed from a single base seed
recorded in the configuration, and every random draw in the model consumes that
seeded stream, so a replicate reproduces exactly given its configuration, its
position in the sweep, and its repeat index. The runs reported here were produced
before the base seed was introduced, and before two corrections to the model —
mortality is now drawn once per step rather than twice for farming breeds, and
immigrant paddy groups are now placed on paddy-arable rather than rainfed-arable
cells. They are being regenerated under the corrected, seeded code. The full parameter set, all submodels, and the design concepts are
specified in the Supplementary Information.
